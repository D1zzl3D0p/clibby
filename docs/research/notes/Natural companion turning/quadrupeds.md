# Quadruped turning: animal evidence and procedural animation implications

## How do limbs and contacts redirect a real animal?

### Takeaway
Measured turns redistribute work between limbs and continue through stance, limb rotation, and successive placements. A fixed flat-foot orientation plus a rigid-body yaw allowance per lifted foot is not an animal-derived rule.

### Cited Findings
- **Measured, dogs jumping while turning:** Söhnel et al. (2021) recorded 20 adult agility Border Collies with eight force plates and sixteen cameras. Landing forelimbs produced higher lateral forces than hindlimbs; outer limbs produced approximately twice the inner limbs' vertical and lateral forces. This is a jump-turn result, not a universal ordinary-walk prescription. [Primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34411999/). A full-paper repository URL was found, but fetching it failed; do not attribute details absent from the abstract to verified full text: [repository PDF](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10335341).
- **Measured, tight walking turns in horses:** Chateau, Degueurce and Denoix (2005), four horses with invasive ultrasonic measurements, found inside-forelimb adduction during stance and rotations in digital joints. Those movements peaked at heel-off, then decreased during breakover while the hoof rotated suddenly laterally. This is direct evidence that stance and turning need not mean an immobile, flat, rigid foot frame. Hoof mechanics do not establish the amount of permissible paw swivel in a dog/cat. [Primary abstract, correct publication year and DOI](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15651728/).
- **Measured, walking circles in horses:** Egenvall et al. (2020) found smaller inner than outer cannon-bone angular excursion: unridden hindlimbs approximately 53° versus 56°. Straight walking hind excursion lay between inner and outer circular-walk values; straight forelimb excursion was greatest. These are segment excursions, not toe-displacement distances. [Primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33240661/).
- **Measured, paws:** A study of 19 clinically sound dogs plus orthopedically affected groups measured mediolateral and craniocaudal movement of center of pressure within the paw throughout stance. Pressure transfer inside a contact patch is therefore a real phenomenon; this does not itself prove whole-paw sliding or yaw rotation. [Primary paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7459534/).

### Inferences
- For animation, keep a stance paw's **position** reasonably stable while allowing the supported limb, body, toes/contact patch and eventual breakover to articulate. Treat positional planting and foot orientation as separate constraints. A controlled toe/edge roll or short unloading swivel is more defensible than keeping a flat paw at its original yaw through an entire reversal; exact amplitudes need visual references.
- Expect shorter inner and larger outer placements on a traveling arc, but avoid asserting that all inner paws universally have longer stance. Relative timing depends on gait, task, surface and limb. Stronger outer force does not by itself establish longer outer stance.
- Give fore and hind body sections distinguishable redirection roles; do not let all four paws chase one rigid rectangular footprint at once. Modest body bend and staggered recovery are useful animation choices, not quantified cat/capybara turning laws.

### Gaps
- No verified ordinary-walk dog/cat/capybara 180° dataset was found here that prescribes a universal first foot, exact inner/outer stance ratio, crossing sequence, or number of steps.
- A search surfaced a computational paper claiming strategy-dependent inner/outer duty-factor changes. It is **model output**, not measured animal evidence, and must not be used to fill that gap: [Speed-Dependent Turning Strategies in Quadrupedal Locomotion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12871115/).

## How do head, spine, forequarters and hindquarters participate in a reversal?

### Takeaway
Body orientation, direction of travel, and contact changes are related but distinct. Evidence supports coordinated whole-body redirection; a universal quantified head-then-shoulders-then-pelvis schedule for ordinary quadruped reversals was not verified.

### Cited Findings
- **Measured, high-speed dog field turns:** Haagensen et al. (2022) found maximal speed decreased with tighter radii and higher angular velocity. Dogs commonly oriented their body ahead of the changing travel direction: mean body over-rotation was about 15.9° with substantial variation (SD 24.9°), increasing with heading change. This supports separating body yaw from instantaneous travel heading; it does not supply slow-turn timing. [Primary full paper, results pp. 7–10](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article-pdf/225/21/jeb244435/2300877/jeb244435.pdf).
- **Measured observations, specialized 180° flyball box turn:** The 2024 3-D study describes braking to reverse travel, forelimb/inner-hind contact while trunk rotation remains unfinished, later outer-hind contact, and hindlimb push-off while forelimbs drop away. This is coordinated near-simultaneous contact plus fore/hind redirection, rather than four isolated foot-yaw transactions. The box, ball retrieval and jump make it a specialized example; its joint angles must not be copied into casual flat-ground turning. [Primary full paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-78863-9).
- **Measured, cats walking straight:** Zubair et al. (2016) found small rhythmic 3-D head motions coupled to stride and concluded that the head is actively held; head control is not just a passive rigid extension of the trunk. This experiment used a straight path and does **not** establish anticipatory head lead during a turn. [Primary paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4986613/).

### Inferences
- An economical procedural turnaround can be organized as a few **coordinated placement beats**, with overlapping body rotation: (1) look/neck and shoulder intent plus a compact inward placement; (2) wider outer placement and continuing forequarter redirection while the hindquarters catch up; (3) recovery/alignment into departure. This is an animation proposal, not a measured universal three-step animal maneuver. Allow the current gait phase to determine the actual first foot.
- Use a small head/neck lead for readable intention and a flexible shoulder-to-pelvis orientation difference; let pelvis catch up before departure. Do not justify a numeric lead delay using human turning experiments or feline aerial-righting research.
- Preserve fast root travel on approach/departure and through feasible broad arcs. For a compact 180° reversal, briefly reduce translational speed or tighten the pivot region while yaw progresses smoothly; then promptly recover travel. Constant high speed, zero-radius reversal and perfect paw planting are conflicting constraints, not a reason to subdivide the body yaw into dozens of tiny steps.
- A useful control split is desired travel path, body yaw, shoulder/pelvis shape, gait phase, and foot targets. Each swing lands toward the *predicted* body/path state at touchdown; plant recovery should occur before reach exhaustion. None requires a full dynamics simulation.

### Gaps
- Ordinary quadruped head-to-trunk-to-pelvis lead delays and 180° reversal step counts remain unverified. Do not present human 0.2–0.6 s anticipatory head rotation as a cat/dog measurement.
- No capybara-specific turning kinetics or paw-swivel limits were located; dogs and horses are analogs with material morphology/task differences.

## Why does strict one-foot-at-a-time yaw look mincing, and what changes with speed?

### Takeaway
Walking does not imply three planted feet throughout every moment, and gait coordination changes with speed. Serializing every swing and using tiny whole-body yaw increments can artificially force cautious short steps and suppress continuous root movement.

### Cited Findings
- **Measured, Labrador walk:** At 0.60–0.90 m/s a portable-walkway study measured mean stance fractions of 55.6% in forelimbs and 50.2% in hindlimbs. Such fractions are far below the 75% average duty factor imposed by a four-leg cycle with exactly one leg swinging at every instant and three always planted. [Primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20807137/).
- **Measured, dog coordination:** A motion-capture study found speed-dependent changes in limb coupling, including some dogs moving toward nearly simultaneous ipsilateral fore/hind placements. Do not impose one immutable coordination pattern on all speeds and animals. [Primary full paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2063503/).
- **Comparative gait evidence:** A 244-species study defines walking by duty factor above 50%, distinguishes lateral/diagonal sequence and couplet patterns, and illustrates the capybara as a lateral-sequence diagonal-couplet example. Its illustrative diagrams are normalized to 60% duty factor, not measured universal capybara contact fractions. [Primary paper and methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8370795/).

### Inferences
- **Arithmetic, not a biological finding:** If only one of four equal-cycle legs may swing, each can swing for at most a quarter cycle; hence average duty factor is at least 0.75. If body yaw is also allowed only in small batches per swing, the model imposes many support resets and many short swing/settle events before completing 180°. This explains a mincing cadence without needing a claim that animals never use cautious single-foot stepping.
- Permit limited swing overlap or near-coupled placements as cadence rises, with support transfer continuing through the turn. A brisk walk or trot should not inherit a slow cautious scheduler's global swing mutex. A trot-like diagonal pairing is a stylistic option; verify the chosen character's gait from video rather than labeling any faster cycle a trot.
- For a low-cost animation, plan a short maneuver horizon: root path and smooth yaw advance continuously; each leg releases/replants on gait phase plus reach urgency; outer targets cover a larger arc, inner targets remain compact; modest shoulders/pelvis offsets absorb the lag. Use a few useful placements and a final alignment step instead of distributing an arbitrary total yaw equally across every foot.
- Let stance feet lift when their anchors approach the leg's usable reach; choose the new target ahead of the predicted hip/shoulder at touchdown. Excessive root stopping, late target updates and short yaw budgets can create tiny steps even when the swing curve itself is smooth.

### Gaps
- The findings do not establish a safe maximum simultaneous swing count for a cartoon capybara, a natural turn duration, or a numeric stride/yaw budget. Those require character geometry and explicit visual evaluation.
- No battery, desktop-parity, or runtime-performance conclusions follow from these biomechanics references.
